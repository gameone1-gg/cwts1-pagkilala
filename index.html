Part 2 // Reflective Essay

Before this course, I thought of citizenship as paperwork. You are born Filipino, you have the IDs, you vote once you are old enough, and that is about it. The first two modules did not make me throw that idea out, because the law really does settle the question: Article IV of the Constitution is where it says who is a Filipino citizen (1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines, 1987). What changed is where I place it. Legal status is the starting point, and not the whole answer. The Citizenship Training lecture says it directly, that citizenship is more than legal status (NSTP Diliman Office, 2020), and that left me with a more useful question: what am I doing with it? The place where I first had to ask that question long before I knew it was a question, was my alma mater, ICAS Marcos Highway.

ICAS is the community in my portrait, and I still consider myself part of it as a graduate. The CWTS Module 1 reminded me that a community is not only a place. It can also be formed through shared experiences, interests, and relationships (Center for Community Health and Development, n.d.-b). That fits, because what holds ICASIANs and BOSCONIANs together is not really the building. It is the people: the students, the administrators, the teachers (including those from the McCoy branch), the parents, the Alpha Phi Omega collegiate members, and my peers. I will never personally know every ICAS graduate, yet I feel connected to them anyway. That is a small version of what the lecture, drawing on Benedict Anderson, calls an imagined community (NSTP Diliman Office, 2020). If the idea works for a school, it makes sense that it works for a country.

When I looked at ICAS more deliberately, the strengths were easy to list, but one of them I had been misreading. The school is relatively niche, and I would once have described that as a weakness: small and not widely known. The Community Tool Box treats people, places, services, and the skills of members as assets (Center for Community Health and Development, n.d.-a). Seen through that lens, I had to admit the smallness is the reason the bonds formed the way they did. Events and ordinary school days felt like being around family, with a kind of “pakikisama” that is hard to create on purpose. The school gave me a welcoming environment to grow in, as a person and not only as a student. My teachers and the APO collegiate members were a part of that, and they had real roles in how my UP exams went. None of that would show up on a standard list of facilities.

Concerns are harder to write about without overclaiming, so I want to separate what I observed from what I can actually say. From where I stood, much of the guidance I received came from a handful of specific people. That was my experience. I cannot say it was every student’s, and I did not gather any data on it, only my first hand impression. But if it is true more widely, support that depends on a few individuals is fragile, since it lasts only as long as those people are around. I am not proposing anything here; I am only noting that the thing I am most grateful for may also be the thing the community should be careful with.

My sense of being Filipino was shaped less by textbooks than by habits I picked up from the people around me. One rule I lean into is the Golden Rule: to treat others as I would want to be treated, which is what I use most often when I am unsure what the morally right choice is. Asking guidance from people who went ahead of me, who reached good decisions through planning, preparation, and some trial and error, is another habit. All of those treat decisions as something shared instead of purely personal. That lines up with how Menguin (2025) connects being a mabuting mamamayan with bayanihan, respect, and contributing to the common good. But the module also asks us to question that reading, and I think I should. Helping is not automatically enough. Someone can volunteer often and still not respect the people they are serving, or act with good intentions and poor judgment (Mangubat, 2026b). The rights and responsibilities also read differently now.

Article II, Section 13 of the Constitution gives the youth a role in nation-building and in public and civic affairs, and I used to read lines like that as slogans (1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines, 1987). The rights in Article III (expression, access to information, association) and the suffrage in Article V now look to me like tools meant to be used, not just protections to be aware of. Berti (2015) makes the point that recognizing rights is easier than making sure people can actually enjoy them. I felt a small version of that. I could go after my UP applications because people around me made it practical. A right on paper and a right you can use are different things, and in my case the difference was other people. Not every student has that kind of support, and I should not assume they do.

My personal goals are fairly practical. I try to work smart instead of just working hard, so I do not repeat mistakes other people already made. Two books I read online, and still re-read each year, helped me use my time and effort better and left me with more free time and rest. In my valedictory speech, I recommended them to my fellow graduates, one on memory and one on social skills, and that is probably my clearest present contribution: passing along what helped me. As an Iskolar ng Bayan, I carry an obligation to give back to the public who funds my education. That is why this is the part I need to examine. Module 1 tells us to know before we assume and observe before we propose (Mangubat, 2026a), and both are slow. Community work cannot always be optimized, because trust takes time and cannot be skipped. If I keep treating everything as a problem of saving time, I risk treating people as resources instead of persons with their own knowledge and agency. That is a limitation I would rather name now than discover halfway through a project.

Some of my assumptions were affirmed, others challenged. What was affirmed is that my progress was never mine alone, and that a good support system makes a real difference. What was challenged is the idea that small means lacking, and the idea that learning from other people’s experience is enough by itself. What was complicated is my picture of a good citizen. I had imagined someone who helps, follows the rules, and votes. Module 2 argues that good citizenship cannot simply mean obedience, and that it includes staying informed, participating, and respecting people whose experiences differ from mine (Mangubat, 2026b). Helping is only one piece of that.

Pagtataya, as the course frames it, is putting something of yourself at stake for something larger than yourself (Mangubat, 2026a). At this point in CWTS 1, mine is modest. It is my time, and it is the efficiency I value so much. It is also showing up for ICAS after graduating, when nothing requires me to, and being honest about what I do not yet know. What I might contribute is what I already do: sharing what I learned, and helping those who come after me prepare. The conditions matter, though. I should listen first and ask what the community already has before offering anything. I should also remember that what worked for me may not fit someone else. Dangal, husay, and paglilingkod are the three ideas the course keeps returning to, and I used to treat them as a sequence, with skill first and service after. Now I think dangal has to come first, since respect is what makes the other two worth anything. I do not yet know what pagtataya will cost me. That uncertainty is probably part of what the word is asking me to accept.
