Part 1 - Community Snapshot and Positionality

Before I started to observe the Mom’s pageantry presence as a candidate representing the Filcom UAE community in the Mrs. Universe Official 2026, I had previously believed that pageants were held on stages and my mother’s involvement with the pageant began after she arrived at the venue. However, Pagmamasid challenged me to see this in a new way: identify the community, state my position within that community, and observe before drawing conclusions (Mangubat, 2026b). I choose the community of the Filcom UAE delegation who joined in Mrs. Universe Official 2026 as well as the people that surrounds and around them - the other contestants, the organizers and production team, emcees and pageant judges, sponsors, audiences and viewers through Facebook reels and live streams. All took place in Metro Manila and neighboring areas from October 12-17, 2026.

The private hotel in Makati is where she stayed for official residence, SM Center San Pedro in Laguna where the sashing ceremony and talent competition was held and the Matrix Creation Events Venue in Quezon City for the grand coronation night, public posts also reveal the visit of the candidates at a correctional institution in Mandaluyong, Metro Manila. I would also show the pre-week days around Antipolo, UP Diliman and a mutual friend’s hotel. I only show my boundary for the Filcom UAE delegation and whom they interacted with and not the entire pageant.

I’m not a candidate. My mother, Regina Quiba, was the candidate for Mrs. Universe, representing the Filcom UAE community and I was on standby from the time we began preparing for the pageant through the presentation of her talent routine, when I was dance partner, up to the moment we left. I can sort of be the participant-observer who is close to the delegation, yet not a member of any other ones, but I was emotionally attached to the event and conscious of where I was in the scene- something I’ll aim to remember next time I watch what I think to be “great.” I had four assumptions for the week: that pageants are only superficial (or rather looks-centric) and that the human rights advocacy was just an “issue theme,” that a week is enough time for a candidate to get together a good preparation, and that candidates from different countries would be decent to each other. The first two I confirmed to be false.

I was able to witness, what I could expect to see; preparation days, the talent contest, the makeup room, the various rehearsal rooms I was permitted to sit in, and the coronation night, as a spectator. What remained beyond my reach, the judging, the interviews with closed doors, the decisions of the organizers, the financial aspect of the event, the clandestine experience of all the delegations, as well as the charity visits and the Human Rights Forum that I read about only in media publications, which I experienced secondhand.

Part 2 - Observation Journal

I kept three observation episodes. For each one I tried to write down what I observed first, then for what I thought it could be, and I put a question mark where I can’t tell.

Episode 1. Days preparing

A few days before the week of the pageant, from about 4:00AM until I went to bed in the evening, in an Antipolo market, in a hotel where one of our mutual friends had stayed, and in UP Diliman, upper Antipolo, and the open space near the Antipolo church by Hinulugang Taktak. It started with fitting and borrowing try on gowns, showwears and accessories at the Antipolo market and borrow from places inside Metro Manila; book a hotel with a mutual friend. The next day: 4:00AM wake up, book a hotel with a mutual friend, go through hundreds of gowns for different purposes, such as arrival wear, performance, talk with mutual friend about game plan at UP Diliman, go gown shopping at upper Antipolo, and find open space to practice. End up practicing the dance “Fight Song” with choreography matched to the lyrics in the open space below the Antipolo church while my mom rehearses her Q&A answers/points on human rights law. Afterwards, we came home in the evening to for my mom to pack for the one week stay at hotel sponsored by the organizers for the candidate.

Overall, we relied on an informal network (mutual friends, rental shops, market sellers) instead of one formal system, tasks overlapped and depended on each other, moved from place to place, and public open space became practice space. And for most of all, the time constraint. In about a week, the candidates had to do a national costume, gown, talent, Q&A, and stage presence, and research articles of the human rights law. I think it takes six months to prepare for this level of pageant so one week is very short. That is just my impression from our experience - I do not know what timeline the organizers or other candidates had. I also wonder how candidates from abroad are able to prepare and how much money was put into the preparation by sponsors and by the candidates. This is just how our delegation prepared, seen from our support point of view, so I cannot more or less say that this is how everyone prepared.

Episode 2. Sashing ceremony and talent competition

This was October 14, 2026 at SM Center San Pedro, Laguna where we arrived from via bus. When I was reunited with mom after her hotel stint, I also met candidates from other countries. In the makeup room, the candidates relaxed with one another before the talent segment, occupying the space with the practice, formation, and preparations of gowns, attires, and make-up around us. We performed our “Fight Song” dance in the mall space while the hosts were preparing the stage, and the crowd responded positively as we left the stage. On the public posting of the organizers, it was a combination of sashing and talent competition, officiated by the owner and the reigning queen. A lot was happening at once, but the candidates’ spirit remained friendly and collaborative rather than wary and suspicious, and their performances was rightfully pertinent towards the human rights theme via song or message. I do wonder whether the relative friendliness of the group was largely a function of them staying together in the same house, and also whether the audience in the mall was there specifically to see the pageant or just there shopping and took a detour to watch.

I only saw the makeup room and one part of the schedule, so I am only aware of decisions backstage made by the event organizer and other candidates’ efforts to prepare. Other days that I was not present I can only speak about based on public posts and what my mother told me. On Day 1, they arrived at a hotel called Privato Makati. Day 4 they took a trip to a salon and aesthetic clinic in Pasig City. Day 6 they went on a two-hour long Human Rights Forum with interview private, headshots, and evening gown photo shoot. Public posts also documented visits to correctional facilities as charity events, where candidates explained the 30 rights of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. I am treating these as reports, and not my own account of witnessing.

Episode 3. Grand coronation night

I was in the venue for the event held on October 16, 2026, at the Matrix Creation Events Venue, Quezon City, with the event programme announced at 6:00 PM. I viewed the official public livestream afterwards on facebook to validate the programme I remembered. The venue played the organizer jingle repeatedly a dozen or more times before the programme began, the audience was told to be quiet repeatedly, and table numbers were called one-by-one for dinner. The programme began with a prayer and the singing of the national anthem, then the twelve judges and their brief pageant/public career profile were introduced, the rest of the programme was then: candidate introductions, national costume competition, host introduction, recognition award presentations to special guests and ambassadors, grand production number, “Best in Talent” (Bronze, Silver, Gold) group of finalists, presentation of recognition awards to sponsors, presentation of candidates’ short human rights messages, and evening gown competition.

Throughout the program the hosts returned to “beauty with a purpose” and human rights, although they appeared to spend varying amounts of time on this. (My mom’s human rights message was in the video segment.) My mom also connected her human rights message to the video segment. She said that human rights are everyone’s birthright and upholding them upholds all of humanity. My mom’s Q & A speech finished to a roar of applause, and I yelled “Mama ko iyan!” in the gallery. The previous Miss Universe Official, Sugar Mercado, was also there, so I had her take a picture with my mom. My Mom’s official statement about why she entered it provides context to what I saw. Her national costume contains the colors of the UAE Filipino community and the Philippine flag (red, green, white, and black, with blue and gold ‘rays of the sun’). As a wife of a seafarer working as an Overseas Filipino Worker (OFW), she said it depicts the sacrifices of OFWs, their spouses, and the ‘rays of the sun’ represents spouses. Her statement explains what she said in her explanation of her Right to Equality; she said no one is ‘more human’ than anyone else and thus everyone should be treated equally with dignity and respect. She received the Best in Smile (Photogenic) award, to which she said she had considered not joining because it requires resources she didn’t have, but the sponsors made it possible, and so she decided to enjoy the experience rather than pursue the award. My biggest questions are what caused the delay before the program and how the Ambassador recognition is awarded.

Zoom back out to the entire map, and the resources are not money, bureaucracy, and regulation; they are the relationships and the places in which we are meant to go to talk to each other, to work through our problems. Part 4 - Stakeholder, Participation, and Inclusion Lens

Regarding the stakeholders, I borrowed the community tool box idea that people who are closely involved in a community initiative tend to have different interests, experience, and levels of power (Center for Community Health and Development, n.d.). I was involved with candidates and their support groups, production staff, hosts, the pageant owner and pageant chairperson, judges (for scoring), and audience members. The potential audience, who are established group of official, formal authority are the owners of the pageant and the pageant director, plus the creative director and the judges (for voting). The informal power is through people we’ve known or been introduced to through mutual friends who can open doors to other people or places we may need. The supporting organizations are the pageant organizer and production company, Privato Hotel Makati, SM Center San Pedro, Matrix Creation Events Venue, and other partner groups as mentioned in public shows of the charity visits. The table indicates how visible each of the groups was to me, which I find interesting because it sort of distinguishes how much of these groups I’ve actually interacted with as opposed to simply having heard of.

Most of the invisible issues are issues that I can only describe - I can’t actually see them. There are all sorts of women that those who “could” have joined, or who “had” to buy in or take leave or find a way to go to Manila from other countries or whatnot, in my opinion would not have been there, so that is a question, not data. The list of questions of inclusion seem long. Economic, costumes and travel cost money. Geographic, the delegates ranged from the Philippines to abroad. Age, in that a former invited awardee was 69, so we know we didn’t exclude anyone by not being “young,” it fits in with their advocacy for women empowerment along with human rights. Language, the program used Filipino and English. Digital, Facebook and even Youtube leads the door in that direction for anyone with access. The information jumped between Facebook posts and reels from the organizer pages, held events through Facebook and Youtube live, was cast by host announcements, the website to which the voting was focused, and a printed schedule of the week, and within our group was coordinated through chats and calls with mutual friends.

Part 5 - Resilience, Environment and Digital Context Lens

This is not a single lens community; so I am going to just point to what I did see. I was not physically threatened; my threat was overload: Too many quick change-outs all over the city, time it takes to get from one venue to another. I also felt in relation to the physical space, the open space in front of the Antipolo church turned rehearsal hall, the mall turned concert hall, the feeling I get in my soul when I change my look or rent costume/dance masks from a sister group to take advantage of a space when there is no special studio for dancing; resilient and resourceful practice of borrowing, renting, trusting friends when there is no physically nearby costume/dance mask change of clothes/space for dancing and team flow is maintained by the common purpose. It has also brought to mind another form of tech participation; dance costume couriered and delivered via motorcycle, online voting, reels, and livestreaming which helps this dance troupe to reach audiences in remote locations. I think of the online viewer and the event viewer as two related, but separate audiences that I’m calling a relative community; but I was not able to observe the experience of those online viewers directly, so I can’t say what it was like for them, but it received a lot of comment from friends and family. Existing capacities were dance or performance skills and contacts and events; and preserving existing networks; and a set message for the cause. The cause message and those digital visits to the different government agencies described in online posts qualify under the definition of civic engagement in Module 3, to not be limited to political engagement, and that volunteerism can be a kind of civic engagement (Mangubat, 2026a).

Part 6 - Critical Observation Reflection

Other than the obvious thing, which I had only noticed because I was walking slower, were the invisible things. When I looked at the pageant day, I just had the gowns, the stage. But in slowing down, I saw the preparaation, the waiting. The planning, the coordination, the travel and logistics, all the crunching of numbers. That weakened certain of my assumptions. I assumed the candidates would be suspicious of strangers, but in the makeup room they were happy to share the space so that weakened; that was not the issue. I assumed that good planning would consume one week, and that was true, but the dearth of time pushes up the effort and the cost; that weakened. I assumed that the program could simply be an arts theme; sure, there are the human rights messages, the section that advocates, but again I just could not see it that deep; that weakened. I assumed the effort was all on the candidate; but that weakened with friends and family, the rental sellers, and so on.

Those people are the strength I initially overlooked – of friends, public spaces, of potential.

Due to my observations, I must write cautiously. I saw candidates, support teams, the audience. I did not see the support of the organizers, the judges’ discussion, the Filipino OFW families in which the national costume was representative, were only to be heard of from my mom’s account. The time and costs dilemma was only heard from one delegation, and the glamour with the content of advocacy is only my impression; thus, the tentative conclusion. I can only conclude that the Filcom UAE delegation depended on the networks and the space for the preparations, and the preparations was pressed for time. However, I cannot conclude that all contestants were facing similarly, that the pageant is not enough for advocacy, and that the organization is not enough efficient.

That is also the reason I wouldn’t jump into a service idea. I would want to hear the organizers, other candidates, and families’ opinions first, because I want to know what the community feels it needs; and about the group’s current needs, who or what is already doing support work with the community, because with a community, one starts with what the community already has (Mangubat, 2026b). In my role, I was a supporter and what has first hand access but third hand bias. I bring an emotional and systemic bias, and tendency to seek optimization and system, and have to hold back and describe first. If I have my service trait at all, it would be for coordination/logistics-- but after I truly understand what the community itself has and wants.
